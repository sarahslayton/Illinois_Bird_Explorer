## Species Description
The Red-tailed Hawk is a large hawk often seen conspicuously perched on fence posts and utility poles or sailing effortlessly overhead along highways. The rusty-red tail of the adult birds is a prominent identification feature. Red-tailed Hawks are common and widespread in North America where they breed throughout Canada, the U.S., and much of Mexico and Central America. Historically, populations have increased in range as forests were cleared for agriculture (Preston and Beane 1993). The Red-tailed nests in open areas with interspersed woodlands, isolated trees in the middle of fields, urban areas where prey is plentiful, and often in rural woodlots and forests where the nest is near the forest edge but can be well hidden. The Red-tailed Hawk tends to nests in smaller and less dense woodlands and closer to human dwellings than the Red-shouldered Hawk (Bednarz and Dinsmore 1982).

## Fast Facts
### Rangewide Distribution:
all of North America from south of the Arctic through Central America
### Illinois Abundance:
common migrant, summer resident and winter resident
### Conservation Status:
None
### Diet:
rabbits, squirrels, mice, birds, reptiles, and amphibians
### Breeding Habitat:
wide variety of habitats from woodlands to open country with scattered trees

## Phenology
### Nest:
bulky platform of sticks and twigs lined with strips of bark and leaves, in a tree
### Eggs:
2–3, white to bluish white, often spotted with brown
### Incubation Period:
30–35 days
### Time to Fledge:
about 45 days

## Illinois Population Trends
### History
During the late nineteenth century, the Red-tailed Hawk was very common in most portions of Illinois, but not as common as the Red-shouldered Hawk (Ridgway 1889). The Red-tailed Hawk continues to be a common and widely distributed breeding species that has been reported nesting statewide. In addition to the breeding population, there are distinct wintering and migratory populations; the interrelationship of these populations is not yet understood.
